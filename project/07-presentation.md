# Checkpoint Seven: Presentation in Class

Your presentation will essentially tell the same story as your narrative analysis, but it will be out loud in class. At this point, your project should be finished enough to describe in some detail, so the remaining task is communicating those findings in  ten minutes to an audience that has not seen your data/notebooks thus far.

The audience is simply all of us in CS 418. We know the basic ideas from this course, but less about your specific topic. [*The Art of Data Science*](https://bookdown.org/rdpeng/artofdatascience/chapter-communication.html) has good advice on this front: try to lead with what you found and why it matters. There's no need to teach us everything about your domain, or walk us through all the technicalities of your methods, so keep the technical details in the background (i.e. in appendix slides) until someone asks about them.

## What to do

1. **Rehearse the story you already have.** Take the two to four findings from your narrative analysis, in the order you chose, one figure each. It's probably best to not start new analysis for the talk, although you might decide to drop a finding or two depending on which ones are strongest / most interesting for the class.
2. **Aim for one point per slide.** At roughly a minute per slide, ten minutes fits about nine slides. Here is a template you can work with, though you are not required to use it. Whichever shape you choose, try to make the title of each slide state its point, e.g. "Late-night trips are twice as likely to be cancelled."
	* Title slide: your project title and every member's name.
	* Research questions: what you asked, and why it matters.
	* Data: what you have, and where it came from.
	* One slide per finding, each with one polished figure sized to be readable from the back of the room.
	* Limits: what your data cannot support, and the question you would ask next.
	* Next steps: what you would do with more time.
	* Keep it visual: no code, screenshots of dataframes, or tables of numbers.
3. **Speaking contributions.** You can divvy up the speaking roles however you like. If multiple people are speaking, be sure to rehearse the hand-offs/transitions, and make sure you know who's up first (and who's up last).
4. **Include "non-findings."** At least one slide should mention limits of your analyses and/or dead-ends that you hit during the semester. This could include alternative explanations that you could not rule out, who or what is missing from the data, and/or questions you would explore with more time. Again, this should be similar material as your narrative analysis, just in presentation format.
5. **Answer questions with evidence.** You will have a few minutes to respond to questions. Try to point to the specific figure, the number, the model, etc. that directly answers the question. "We did not check that" is an acceptable answer.
6. **Rehearse timing.** The talk gets ten minutes; questions come out of the fifteen minutes each group has in the class period. Time yourselves, and aim to finish around 9 minutes - 9:30 a slow start does not cost you the ending.


## What to submit

One submission per group in Canvas: a **link to your slides**, some time before your presentation, but ideally by 11:59pm the night before your assigned presentation day. Any group member can submit.

Nothing else is due for this checkpoint. The talk itself is graded in class, and individual contribution will be graded separately in the peer assessment checkpoint.

## Rubric

Four levels: Excellent (1), Good (0.8), Adequate (0.6), Developing (0.4). The descriptions adapt language from the rubrics listed under [Sources](#sources).

| Category | Excellent (1) | Good (0.8) | Adequate (0.6) | Developing (0.4) |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Story | Within the first couple of minutes, the audience knows what you asked and why it is interesting or important. The research questions are clearly stated, each finding addresses one of them and builds on the one before it, and the conclusions are clear. | The research questions are stated, and the findings address them in a sensible order with a clear opening and close. One or two findings may not clearly connect to the research questions. | The research questions are stated, but some findings only partly address them, or the talk simply follows the order in which you did the work (as opposed to the order that best answers the questions). | The research questions are unclear, and the talk walks through the work as a catalog, so the audience cannot say what you found. |
| Evidence | Each finding is connected to a figure that shows its key numbers, and the audience can tell which comparison or model produced them. Each number comes with a spoken explanation/interpretation. | Same as Excellent, but one or two explanations might br missing or unclear in some way. | Some findings carry a figure and a number while others are stated without support, so the audience cannot see where all of the numbers/findings came from. Some numbers are read aloud without saying what they mean. | Findings are asserted without a number, numbers appear that the audience cannot trace to an analysis, and/or the speaker reads lists of numbers off the slides. |
| Limits | Limitations are carefully articulated. The presentation identifies what the data cannot support (e.g. a confounder, a gap in scope, a missing group, etc.), the difficulties you ran into and how you handled them, and the question(s) you would ask if you had more time. | Limitations are noted on their own slide along with what it would take to address them, though some claims might reach beyond the stated limits, or even subtly contradict them. | Limitations appear only as a caveat at the end or a bullet on another slide, with little explanation of which claims they affect or what you would do next. | Limitations are glossed over or left out entirely, or the talk claims more than the data supports. |
| Delivery | Presenters speak clearly and loud enough for the back row, looking at the audience, and using notes minimally. Any transitions/hand-offs are smooth, the talk finishes within ten minutes, and each question gets a direct answer. | Presenters are mostly clear and audible, and the talk finishes within ten minutes. Presenters sometimes look at the slides more than the room, and some questions are not directly addressed. | Presenters are sometimes hard to hear or unclear, hand-offs might stall, or presenters mostly read from slides or notes. Timing might be a little off, and some questions might only get a partial answer. | The talk runs well past ten minutes, presenters are hard to hear throughout or read the slides aloud, and/or questions are deflected. |
| Visuals | Every figure is legible from the back row, with labeled axes and a clear, descriptive title. Slides carry only the text and figures that support what is being said, have no spelling or formatting errors, and make sense to audience members who are less familiar with the domain/topic/data. | Figures and text are mostly legible from the room, and most slide titles state a clear point. A few slides have unnecessary text, a figure that is hard to read from the back row, or minor spelling and formatting issues. | Slides are readable on a laptop but crowded from the room. Slides might hjave more than one point, or some content appears just because it was available. | Code, screenshots of dataframes, or tables of numbers appear on slides without clear reasons, and/or text is too small to read from the room. |


## AI/LLM policy

**Yellow over red. Proceed with caution on the main route, stop at the next signal.**

🟡 You may use AI/LLM-based tools for slide mechanics, e.g. formatting figures and captions.

🔴 You should not use AI/LLM-based tools to write the talk, choose which findings make the deck, or prepare answers to questions. The whole point of the presentation is for *you* to communicate about your data and analyses. Also, using a script you did not write is a good way to undermine your confidence during the presentation.

Side note - I have seen about 40 student presentations this semester in my other class, CS 377. Trust me, the audience will know if your presentation is slop. Please put some effort in! We all notice and appreciate it!
