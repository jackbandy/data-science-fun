# Bridges of Königsberg and Shikaakwa — Sources

> _NOTICE: this file, `make_bridges_figures.py`, `shikaakwa-bridges.csv`, and the figures were drafted by an LLM coding system and have not yet been reviewed._

All figures are generated by `make_bridges_figures.py` (run it with `docs/slides/.venv/bin/python3 make_bridges_figures.py` from this folder). Every frame shares one 1000 × 618 canvas, so each set stacks in an r-stack. Intended for the Week 12, Day 1 slides (intro to network analysis).

## Königsberg (`konigsberg-v0` … `v4`)

Redrawn schematically after Figure 1 of Leonhard Euler, "Solutio problematis ad geometriam situs pertinentis," *Commentarii academiae scientiarum Petropolitanae* 8 (1736, publ. 1741): 128–140 ([Euler Archive E53](https://scholarlycommons.pacific.edu/euler-works/53/)). The original is public domain. The bridges keep Euler's letters a–g. The land masses are lettered by the first letter of their names, matching the Shikaakwa set: K Kneiphof (Euler's A), V Vorstadt (B), A Altstadt (C), L Lomse (D). The names come from [Wikipedia, "Seven Bridges of Königsberg"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Bridges_of_K%C3%B6nigsberg), which says the city "included two large islands—Kneiphof and Lomse—which were connected to each other, and to the two mainland portions of the city—Altstadt and Vorstadt—by seven bridges." Altstadt is the north bank (Euler's upper C) and Vorstadt the south bank: Wikipedia's [Vorstadt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vorstadt_(K%C3%B6nigsberg)) article describes land "on the southern shore of the Pregel River". This placement assumes Euler's figure is drawn with north up, which fits Lomse being in the east.

`konigsberg-v3` and `v4` also show the graph's adjacency matrix, with land masses in alphabetical order and each cell counting the bridges between that pair. In v4 a degree column (the row sums) is added, with odd values in orange like the nodes.

The English quote on every frame and the bridge-to-land assignments come from the translation in Norman L. Biggs, E. Keith Lloyd & Robin J. Wilson, *Graph Theory 1736–1936* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1976), pp. 3–8, as excerpted in [this University of Minnesota course handout](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~reiner/Classes/Konigsberg.pdf):

> "in Königsberg in Prussia, there is an island A, called the Kneiphof; the river which surrounds it is divided into two branches, as can be seen in Fig. [1.2], and these branches are crossed by seven bridges, a, b, c, d, e, f and g. Concerning these bridges, it was asked whether anyone could arrange a route in such a way that he would cross each bridge once and only once."

> "If a traveller goes from A to B over bridge a or b, I write this as AB … Thus, if the traveller leaves B and crosses into D over bridge f, this crossing is represented by BD … if the traveller goes on from D to C over the bridge g …"

> "in the Königsberg problem, there were odd numbers attached to the letters A, B, C and D"

## Euler's original (`euler-1741-*`)

`euler-1741-E053.pdf` is the full scan of E53 from the [Euler Archive](https://scholarlycommons.pacific.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1052&context=euler-works) (University of the Pacific Scholarly Commons; the first PDF page is the archive's cover sheet). The two PNGs are that PDF's embedded page scans, extracted losslessly with `pdfimages` at their native 200 dpi. `euler-1741-plate.png` is PDF page 3, the fold-out plate "Comment. Acad. Sc. Tom VIII. Tab. VIII. p. 128" with Figs. 1–3. In this copy someone has handwritten the German bridge names beside Euler's letters in Fig. 1. `euler-1741-p136.png` is printed page 136 (PDF page 11): the bridge-count table for Königsberg (A 5, B 3, C 3, D 3) and the start of §15 on Fig. 3.

`euler-1741-plate-color.jpg` is the same plate from a different physical copy of volume 8: the American Museum of Natural History Library copy, scanned in color at 300 ppi for the Biodiversity Heritage Library ([Internet Archive `commentariiacade08impe`](https://archive.org/details/commentariiacade08impe), marked "NOT_IN_COPYRIGHT"). It is the 2179 × 2020 page image of PDF page 186 ([page view](https://archive.org/details/commentariiacade08impe/page/n185/mode/1up)), downloaded 2026-11-09 from `https://archive.org/download/commentariiacade08impe/page/n185.jpg`. This copy has no handwritten bridge names. In that scan the article itself is PDF pages 154–166, and the fold-out plate is bound later, at PDF pages 185–186.

`euler-1741-fig1-color.jpg` is Fig. 1 alone, cropped from `euler-1741-plate-color.jpg` with `magick euler-1741-plate-color.jpg -crop 1620x728+240+120 +repage -quality 92 euler-1741-fig1-color.jpg`. The top edge sits just below the plate header, so a speck of the "p" in "p. 128" shows at the top right.

Both color images are on the Week 12, Day 1 slide "Euler, 1741": the full plate, then Fig. 1 alone.

## Shikaakwa (`shikaakwa-v1` … `v5`)

**Name.** From George Ironstrack, ["What does the word Chicago mean? (FAQ)"](https://aacimotaatiiyankwi.org/2013/08/01/what-does-the-word-chicago-mean-faq/), *Aacimotaatiiyankwi* (Myaamia Center blog), August 1, 2013:

> "In the Miami-Illinois language, our name for the wild leek plant (Allium tricoccum) is šikaakwa. Historically, the Chicago River had large populations of wild leek and so Myaamia people named the river the Šikaakwa Siipiiwi (The Wild Leek River)."

The FAQ cites Michael McCafferty, "A Fresh Look at the Place Name Chicago," *Journal of the Illinois State Historical Society* 96, no. 2 (Summer 2002): 116–29.

**Map.** River centerlines are OpenStreetMap waterways, © OpenStreetMap contributors, [ODbL](https://opendatacommons.org/licenses/odbl/). They were fetched via the Overpass API on 2026-11-08, clipped to the frame, simplified (Ramer–Douglas–Peucker, 6 m) and hard-coded in the script with their way ids. The frame runs from just north of North Ave to just north of Randolph St, and from the west bank to just west of Wells St. Every walkable bridge inside the frame is drawn.

**Bridges.** `shikaakwa-bridges.csv` is the source of truth. Each row has its OSM way id, and rows with `counted=no` record crossings that were left out and why. Status notes:

- *Cherry Ave (2).* Quinn Kasal, ["The past, present, and future of the Cherry Avenue bike and pedestrian bridge"](https://chi.streetsblog.org/2020/11/16/the-past-present-and-future-of-the-cherry-avenue-bike-ped-bridge/), *Streetsblog Chicago*, Nov. 16, 2020: "it's currently a bike/ped-only facility; and it may become a transitway as part of the Lincoln Yards development."
- *Halsted St (6), Chicago Ave (7).* CDOT, ["Chicago Avenue Bridge and Halsted Street Viaduct"](https://www.chicago.gov/city/en/depts/cdot/supp_info/chicago-avenue-bridge-and-halsted-street-viaduct-.html) (fetched 2026-11-08): "Halsted Street is fully closed in both directions to all traffic." Project area includes "Halsted Street from Ancona Street to the Chicago River". Timeline: "Estimated Completion: Halsted Street: December 2026 Chicago Avenue Bridge: Spring 2027". The figures count both bridges as part of the normal network. Closing 6 in v5 is a modeling choice that makes every degree even; it doesn't depict the current closure.
- *Lake St (11).* OSM tags the road deck as under construction with `opening_date=2028-01-31`, citing a WGN report on a three-year renovation (not independently checked). The Green and Pink Line 'L' runs on the upper deck. It's drawn as a grey track, a nod to the course's transit motif, and it isn't a walking route.

**Portrait** (`shikaakwa-portrait`). The v1 map alone, with no key or graph, on a 453 × 733 golden-ratio portrait canvas, drawn by the same map code. It's on the Week 12, Day 1 slide "Euler's Rule".

**Degrees** (computed by the script, not typed): v4 has W 7, N 8, G 5, L 2. Two odd land masses means an open walk exists from Goose Island to the West Side (or the reverse), but no closed one. v5 closes Halsted St (6), giving W 6, N 8, G 4, L 2. Every land mass is even, so a closed walk exists.

**Fonts.** There are two text styles: the title (Big Shoulders, 38 px) and one small style for everything else (Libre Franklin, 15 px). Titles use Big Shoulders. Its README (`../fonts/big-shoulders/upstream-README.md`) says it was "created for the Chicago Design System, as well as the citizens of Chicago," with forms "based in Chicago's multiple histories in railway transport", which fits a Chicago river-crossing figure. Body text uses Libre Franklin. Both are OFL and live in `../fonts/`.
