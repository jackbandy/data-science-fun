### Care Ethics

Care ethics is a theory of morality focused on concepts of caring: "attentiveness, responsibility, nurturance, compassion, [and] meeting others' needs" [@tronto_moral_1993]. The theory begins from the observation that all humans need care, particularly as we all begin our lives as "helpless children" [@baier1987need], and that that "human progress and flourishing hinge fundamentally on the care that those needing it receive" [@held2006ethics].

Given its emphasis on interdependence and affect, some authors refer to ethics of care as "relational ethics" or "ethics of love." @sanderstaudt_care_2011 also observes that "care ethics, feminine ethics, and feminist ethics are often treated as synonymous." Consistent with the pluralistic nature of feminism more broadly, care ethics is just one branch of feminist ethics [@norlock_feminist_2025; @mackay_feminism_2019], and it traces its origins to the work of psychologist Carol Gilligan.

#### Origins: Gilligan and the Heinz Dilemma

While researching Lawrence Kohlberg's stages of moral development, Carol Gilligan [@gilligan1993different] observed that children scoring "low" on his hierarchy were simply reasoning differently: they prioritized contextual, relational care rather than searching for a definitive "right answer."

The differences are often illustrated through the Heinz dilemma: should a man steal a drug to save his dying wife? Kohlberg ranked children by their *style of reasoning* rather than the answer they gave. Gilligan described how children like "Jake" treated the dilemma as a math problem with a single right answer, while children like "Amy" treated it as a narrative, asking about relationships and proposing solutions such as bartering with the store owner.

Kohlberg's framework did not account for these sorts of questions and approaches, which led Gilligan to theorize about morality as grounded in relationships.

#### Defining Care

Although the notion of care can seem intuitive and accessible, theorists have still pursued formal definitions focusing on care as labor [@held2006ethics], as a virtue [@desautels_feminists_2001], and/or as a broad set of behaviors [@engster_care_2006]. One of the most popular definitions comes from Tronto and Fischer, who view caring as

> "a species of activity that includes everything that we do to maintain, continue, and repair our 'world' so that we can live in it as well as possible" [@tronto_moral_1993].

Tronto identifies four *phases* of caring, which can also be viewed as a definitional framework:

1. attentiveness (noticing the needs of others)
2. responsibility (willingness to respond to those needs)
3. competence (skillfully meeting those needs)
4. responsiveness (empathy, wisdom, and awareness around the wide potential of caring activities)

The theory generally resists "general guidelines, or principles, or a set of out-of-the-box tools" [@birhane2021algorithmic], treating context-sensitivity as a strength. Still, @held2006ethics names five distinctive characteristics associated with caring:

* meeting the needs of particular others;
* embracing emotion;
* rejecting impartial abstraction;
* reconceiving the public/private boundary;
* seeing people as relational and interdependent.

#### Care Ethics and the "Big Three"

The "distinctive characteristics" offered by Virginia Held give us a helpful starting point to contrast care ethics with other prominent ethical theories.

Deontology, a duty-based framework, prizes "impartial, universal, rational moral principles" [@held2006ethics]. Annette Baier describes the deontological approach as "rule worship" [@baier_moral_1995], exemplified by Kant's categorical imperative: the pursuit of rules that should be followed by all people at all times in all places. Care theorists instead frame moral decisions in terms of different relationships with family, neighbors, friends, and communities.

Context-sensitivity also distinguishes care ethics from utilitarianism, which essentially operates on a single rule (minimize pain, maximize pleasure [@bentham_utilitarianism_2004]). According to care theorists, utilitarian ethics are "built on the image of the independent, autonomous, rational individual" [@held2006ethics]. Such theories of justice and morality (including Rawls' famous veil of ignorance [@rawls1971theory]) suggest we operate based on a "view from nowhere," meaning that every life is viewed the same regardless of relationships, histories, or other contextual factors.

Baier offers a poignant contrast to such theories: imagine a city of perfect justice, where nobody harms anyone else, nobody  steals from anyone else, and nobody breaks any laws. She asks us to really consider the people in such a society:

> They may well be lonely... apathetic about their work and about participation in political processes, find their lives meaningless, and have no wish to leave offspring to face the same meaningless existence. Their rights, and respect for rights, are quite compatible with very great misery, and misery whose causes are not just individual misfortune and psychic sickness but social and moral impoverishment. [@baier1987need]

This illustrates the "need for more than justice" to support a truly flourishing society. Care ethicists do not intend to dismiss justice or ignore individual rights, only to shift some ethical attention to matters of human relationships. Tronto proposes "redrawing boundaries" around various theories, not discarding them [@tronto_moral_1993].

Notably, virtue ethics overlaps substantially with care ethics. Some theorists even treat care as one virtue among others: @mclaren_feminist_2001 advocates a feminist virtue ethics in which care works alongside virtues such as playfulness, and @halwani_care_2003 counts care "as one virtue, albeit an important one, among those that go into constituting a flourishing life." @sanderstaudt_unhappy_2006 calls this an "unhappy marriage," since the theories are inversions of one another: "care ethics scrutinizes virtue in the context of how best to achieve the goals of care, while virtue ethics scrutinizes care in the context of how best to achieve virtue and a flourishing life." She argues that virtue ethics and care ethics can interact through "critical collaboration, rather than of submersion or assimilation."

#### Limitations and Critiques

Although Gilligan emphasized that care ethics "is characterized not by gender but theme" [@gilligan1993different], some readings criticize her work as reinforcing gender stereotypes and promoting essentialism [@mackay_feminism_2019]. This includes Elizabeth Spelman's broader critique of feminist thought, which argues any account that treats "woman" as a single kind (i.e. with a shared moral voice) ends up generalizing from the experience of the most privileged women [@spelman_inessential_1988; @norlock_feminist_2025].

Other critiques concern the direction and scope of care ethics. In @davion_autonomy_1993's example of misdirected care, a caregiver punctually serves dinner to someone who "needs dinner at six-thirty sharp in order to make it to a Klan meeting." Prioritizing care for those nearby might also be poor guidance for the privileged and powerful [@friedman_beyond_1987], who can reinforce inequity and cronyism if they care only for those close to them. Finally, Gilligan's sample over-represented privileged groups: the Heinz dilemma reads very differently if Heinz is Black [@walker_race_2004; @alexander_new_2011]. Later care theorists have responded by emphasizing communal and political care alongside individual care [@tronto_moral_1993; @held2006ethics].
